# Let Anyone at Your Company Ship Internal Apps with AI

Garrett Galow of WorkOS describes how AI has made building software cheap but releasing it internally remains hard. An account manager built "Perona GPT," a chatbot trained on her customer calls, using Lovable, but when the CEO tried it he lacked a Lovable account and could not access it. Two engineers from the applied AI team had to port, deploy, and support the app, illustrating that creation is cheap while distribution, authentication, and security still require engineering effort.

WorkOS identified two requirements: everyone in the company should be able to build and release applications without going to the infrastructure department or obtaining security clearance, and apps should be easily distributable via a link that anyone can open in Slack without warnings or bureaucracy. The goal is to avoid wasting engineering time converting every useful prototype into a full deployment, and to prevent non-engineers from mishandling API keys or deployment platforms they don't have accounts for.

To generate feedback and ideas, WorkOS runs company-wide "Cloud Days," one-day hackathons where non-technical employees work directly at the keyboard paired with engineers who act as consultants. This engages marketing and sales people with app ideas, helping them avoid getting stuck and suggesting the best course of action.

- AI makes building internal apps cheap, but deploying, authenticating, and securing them remains an engineering bottleneck.
- Non-engineers often lack accounts on deployment platforms like Vercel or Cloudflare, blocking distribution even when the app works.
- WorkOS aims for anyone to ship internal apps without infrastructure resources or security clearance, and to share them via a simple Slack link.
- Company-wide "Cloud Days" pair non-technical employees with engineers to build apps and gather feedback.
