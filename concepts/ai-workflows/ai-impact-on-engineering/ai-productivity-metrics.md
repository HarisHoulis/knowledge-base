# Fragments: June 2

Martin Fowler's June 2 fragments collect observations on AI's impact on software engineering productivity and practice. Greg Wilson's critique of common AI value metrics (lines of code, tickets, surveys) is noted, with Fowler acknowledging that qualitative signals may be the best available even if flawed [source](https://third-bit.com/2026/05/20/twelve-ways-to-be-wrong/). Benedict Evans counters job-loss fears by showing a century of accounting automation increased the number of accountants, citing Jevons paradox and the shifting nature of jobs, concluding AI's employment impact is nearly impossible to forecast [source](https://www.ben-evans.com/benedictevans/2026/5/24/ai-job-exposure).

- Common AI productivity metrics are flawed, but qualitative indicators can still be useful when better measures are unavailable.
- Historical automation of professions like accounting did not eliminate jobs; it changed the work, making AI job forecasts unreliable.
- Open models are catching up to closed models faster over time, with no clear capability moats.
- AI-generated hallucinations in published reports can poison the internet and mislead future research, as shown by GPTZero's investigation of an Ernst & Young report.
- AI can be used for defense: Mozilla fixed 423 security bugs in April 2026 versus 17-31 per month in 2025.
- Technical debt, organizational alignment, and human attention are becoming the true bottlenecks as building speed increases.
