# The October Inflection Point for Vibe Game Development

Simon Willison argues that October 2026 marks a new inflection point for "vibe coding" computer games, comparable to the November 2025 inflection point for coding agents. Claude Opus 5.5 in particular shows meaningful improvements in the areas needed to build a compelling game, most importantly a sense of what makes a game actually fun to play—something he previously doubted models could achieve. Prompting it to "Make it a LOT more fun" produced real results, and he has since vibe coded four games he genuinely enjoyed playing, with no looking at the code at all.

The four projects illustrate a range of ambition. Tree Appreciator, a three.js spot-the-difference game about algorithmically generated trees, was built in a single shot in the regular Claude chat interface. Emberwick, a top-down fantasy RPG, grew through iterative prompts from a demo engine to a full game with a plot, magic system, and level editor, and required playtesting to fix gameplay bugs. Battle Drones, a Command & Conquer-style RTS, was transformed by Opus 5.5 after being ported to three.js, and now has multiplayer, replays, maps, and a mode where different LLMs play against each other. Quarter Sun Cove, a cosy giant-pumpkin farming simulator, was built from a spec file over nearly two days of token burn and is full of easter eggs.

Willison notes that GPT-6 Astra can build games but its sense of fun doesn't yet match Opus 5.5, and he expects OpenAI to catch up by year's end. He recommends starting with a prompt like "Build a mobile-friendly three.js game where..." in chat interfaces, then switching to Claude Code or Codex past the proof-of-concept phase, and adding "Make it a LOT more fun" if needed. All his games are HTML/JavaScript on static hosting, and he finds mobile web games indistinguishable from native for his purposes.

He acknowledges the wider games community's aversion to AI-generated assets, noting that vibe coded games combine AI-generated art, music, writing, and code at once. He expects a split between players who refuse such games and those who embrace the widened access, and says he won't enter his games in traditional jams because he doesn't feel he's earned it. What excites him is that none of these projects took more than four hours of his own work, and he wants to see games that wouldn't have existed without these tools.
