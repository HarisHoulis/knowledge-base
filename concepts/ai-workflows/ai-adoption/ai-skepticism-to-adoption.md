# Stop Being Skeptical About AI for Development with Charity Majors

In a podcast episode with Gergely Orosz, Charity Majors (CTO of Honeycomb) explains how her view on AI shifted from cautious skepticism in early 2025 to seeing it as a generational change by November 2025. She credits not just model improvements like Opus 4.5 but especially coding harnesses like Claude Code, which turned AI from a proof-of-concept into serious infrastructure. She compares 2025's AI impact to 2010's cloud computing, arguing that the industry has passed a tipping point where AI's role in software development is undeniable (Orosz, 2026).

Majors predicts that engineers will eventually ship code they have not read, and the key question is building systems to validate such code with confidence. She draws an analogy to infrastructure's shift from 'pets' to 'cattle': instead of fixing buggy code, teams may generate new verifyably correct code. She also offers contrarian takes: code review is overrated, and DevOps has failed in its goal to connect developers with production. AI’s non-deterministic outputs demand more engineering discipline, especially around validation (tests, evals, conformance) (Orosz, 2026).

For engineering leaders, Majors advises running toward AI and gaining experience before it becomes a filter in job interviews. She suggests that anxious directors move back to IC roles to reclaim agency. She also recommends small acts of resistance against AI fatigue, like Honeycomb's 'no AI on Wednesdays,' and calls on both AI-pilled and anti-AI camps to honestly tell the whole story—including costs, not just wins (Orosz, 2026).

- AI's impact in 2025 is comparable to cloud computing in 2010; skepticism was rational but is now increasingly untenable.
- Coding harnesses like Claude Code, not just models, are the key enabler making AI a generational shift.
- Engineers will eventually ship code they haven't read; the bottleneck shifts to validation and reliability systems.
- Code review is overrated; humans contribute more to deciding what to build than reading code for bugs.
- AI demands more engineering discipline, not less, and engineering leaders should get AI experience immediately to remain relevant.
