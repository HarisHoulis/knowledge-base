# Grokking the Brain: What AI Reveals About How You Learn

Barbara Oakley argues that the rise of generative AI raises a genuine concern: excessive delegation of cognitive work to AI may "decompose" or degrade our brains, a question she explored while co-chairing a forum in Frankfurt on how the brain copes with tasks delegated to AI. She notes that neuroscience research is fragmented—hippocampus specialists, basal ganglia experts, cognitive psychologists, and edtech specialists rarely talk to each other—so the forum deliberately brought 50 leading researchers from these siloed fields together to understand how to protect our brains in the age of generative AI.

Oakley traces the deep connection between AI and neuroscience: AI pioneers, trying to define intelligence, turned to the smartest system they knew—the human brain—and AI and neuroscience researchers have collaborated closely ever since. She points to the 2017 "Attention is All You Need" paper (co-chaired conference president Terry Sejnowski reportedly didn't think it was anything special at the time) as the origin of the transformer, whose flow of information she sees as remarkably similar to information flow in the human brain.

Her personal story—hating math and science, learning Russian in the army, starting at remedial high-school algebra at 26, and eventually becoming an engineering professor—frames her argument that learning capacity is not fixed. The talk sets up a synthesis of neuroscience and AI to explain how people actually learn and how to keep learning effectively alongside generative AI.

- Excessive reliance on generative AI may "decompose" the brain, motivating neuroscience research into cognitive degradation.
- Neuroscience is siloed—hippocampus, basal ganglia, cognitive psychology, and edtech experts rarely collaborate—so cross-disciplinary forums are needed.
- AI concepts like the transformer were inspired by and resemble information flow in the human brain.
- Oakley's own late start in math (remedial algebra at 26, becoming an engineering professor) illustrates that learning ability is not fixed.
