# The Agent Works. Does Anyone Trust It?

Michele Grant argues that launching an AI agent proves only that the technology functions, not that people will use it or that it will generate profit. Drawing on over 20 years of business transformation and M&A integration experience, she contends that trust must be built before day one through early change and risk management, just as it was for platform launches in past deals. An agent gains trust when it delivers planned business value while staying strictly within the company's approved risk appetite, policies, and regulatory norms.

Leadership must answer three questions: what value does the agent create, who is responsible for it, and what evidence gives it permission to operate with greater autonomy. Once an agent exits the test environment into the main business process, the nature of the test changes—the pilot tests software functionality, but day-to-day work verifies whether it creates value within management's controls. Established AI risk frameworks offer principles but not per-agent application; for each agent, management must decide what it is allowed to do, who owns it, what evidence it records, and what triggers escalation. Grant frames this as an executive operational mandate, not an engineering task.

A worked example: a $15 billion acquisition targeting $400 million in net synergies, managed by an Integration Management Office (IMO) across 40 work areas, where analysts reconcile weekly numbers with the general ledger. An agent automates the first draft of the steering committee report, and after a four-week pilot its drafts match analysts' reports line for line—so the technology works. But the steering committee needs a written value proposition agreed before the agent touches working data, with three metrics: cycle time (e.g., reducing report preparation from five to two days), analyst productivity (e.g., returning 80 hours per week, valuable only when redirected to lagging work streams and net of computation, licensing, and human verification costs), and detection time—the most important—measuring how quickly a deviation in the data reaches the committee. Each metric requires a baseline before deployment; if the IMO cannot name the source data today, don't deploy.

Responsibility is assigned via the three lines of defense model. The integration manager owns the agent's results as she did manually collected data; risk and compliance oversee the agent and review its methods, with vetting depth depending on the agent's risk level—an internal draft reviewed by a senior analyst requires very different treatment than higher-risk uses.

- Launching an agent proves it works, not that people will trust or use it—trust must be built before day one through early change and risk management.
- Leadership must answer three questions per agent: what value it creates, who is responsible, and what evidence permits greater autonomy.
- Value must be measured against baselines for cycle time, analyst productivity (net of computation, licensing, and human verification), and detection time—if you can't name the source data, don't deploy.
- Responsibility follows the three lines of defense: the business owner owns results, while risk and compliance oversee the agent with vetting depth scaled to its risk level.
- Per-agent governance is an executive operational mandate, not an engineering task.
