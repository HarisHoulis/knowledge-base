# The Signal Layer: What to Build When Anything Can Be Built — Lena Hall, Akamai

Lena Hall opens with a world where building is abundant: she resolved a production incident from a hiking trail, and a friend ran 18 agents while biking. But that abundance is not a moat—competitors receive the same leverage the same morning. Because models answer from common knowledge, asking one what users want or what to build next produces the same confident, competent, and identical answer anyone else would get. Hall calls this a 'convergence machine' and argues the single decision it cannot make is where to point it (Hall, 2026, 0:00–5:47).

She rejects the comfortable consolation that taste will save you, because taste is preference under feedback—precisely what these systems learn. What survives is narrower: judgment about things that have not yet happened, since no data exists for them, and judgment inside a relationship the model cannot observe, because it has read everything about a customer but never met them. She invokes Hamming's rule that a problem is important only when you have an attack on it, and notes that agents just handed everyone an attack on everything, making the scarce skill the choice of which problem deserves one (Hall, 2026, 5:47–9:56).

The rest of the talk concerns how signal survives on the way to a customer. Founders compress past legibility, org charts round toward the mean, and a narrow eval becomes a promise. Hall recommends welding the limit to the claim, because trust is the thing with no grader (Hall, 2026, 11:16–18:04).

- Abundant agentic leverage is not a differentiator; competitors get it too (Hall, 2026).
- Models are convergence machines that answer from common knowledge; only humans decide where to point them (Hall, 2026).
- Taste is trainable preference under feedback; judgment about unprecedented events and unobservable relationships is not (Hall, 2026).
- Hamming's attack rule is inverted: agents provide attacks on everything, so choosing which problem deserves one is the scarce skill (Hall, 2026).
- Preserve signal by compressing past legibility, avoiding organizational rounding, and tying evals to the true limit of your claim (Hall, 2026).
