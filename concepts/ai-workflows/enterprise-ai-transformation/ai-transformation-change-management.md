# Your AI Pilots Won't Move the P&L: Change Management Over Engineering

Adam Nahari argues that despite widespread AI adoption—over 55% of American adults have used generative AI and almost 90% of companies report using AI in at least one function—only 6% report a significant impact on EBIT. He compares this to Robert Solow's 1987 quip that the computer age is visible everywhere except in productivity statistics, noting the same pattern is now occurring at larger scale and faster rate. The time savings are real (roughly 2% of total US hours worked) but almost none of it shows up in profit and loss statements.

His core mental model: in every AI transformation he has led or observed, engineering is a small part—roughly one engineering unit for every five change management units, and that ratio shrinks as models improve. The real effort and the usual point of failure lie in choosing the right problem, building consensus, driving implementation, and measuring results.

Nahari distinguishes three levels of AI in business: general productivity tools (co-pilots and chatbots), whose benefits are competed away into pricing rather than margin; industry-specific AI (specialized assistants or search over company documents); and operational AI—vertical systems embedded in core business processes like airline crew scheduling. Companies seeing significant bottom-line impact are three times more likely to redesign their entire workflow end-to-end rather than bolt on a new tool, investing in the third level alongside the first.

He outlines steps for successful transformation. First, pick one or two problems, not forty: start from the income statement (e.g., airline EBITDA driven by routes/demand, aircraft/supply, maintenance/constraint, and crew/cost) and choose an initiative that could radically change the situation. Good use cases affect a line the CFO tracks, have pre-agreed baselines, rest on ready infrastructure and owned data, have a specific individual owner (not a department), and rank among the CEO's top five priorities. Second, rethink the process from-and-to: automating an existing process yields negligible gains, while redesigning around new technology can be transformational—illustrated by replacing manual crew planning with an optimization engine that enumerates and ranks all feasible solutions by cost in under a minute.

- Only 6% of companies report significant EBIT impact from AI despite ~90% adoption; time savings (~2% of US hours worked) aren't reaching P&L statements.
- Change management outweighs engineering roughly 5:1, and the engineering share shrinks as models improve; failures usually occur in problem selection, consensus, and measurement.
- General productivity tools get competed into pricing; durable margin gains come from operational AI embedded in core processes and full workflow redesign.
- Pick one or two high-impact problems tied to CFO-tracked metrics with pre-agreed baselines, a single accountable owner, and CEO-level priority.
- Automating an existing process yields marginal gains; redesigning around new technology (e.g., constraint-aware crew optimization) delivers transformational results.
