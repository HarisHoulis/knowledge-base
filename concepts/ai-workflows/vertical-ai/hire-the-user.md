# Trading Desks to Clinical Trials: Parallels in Applied Vertical AI

Ayush Bhardwaj moved from applied AI at a hedge fund to a pharma tech company and found the job identical, including the core challenge: teams cannot judge whether the AI's output is actually good. Engineers can instantly spot weak code, but they lack the analogous instinct for trade theses or drug candidates. This is where vertical AI projects quietly die—the artifact looks finished, but no one buys it because it doesn't truly work.

Attempting to use a model as the judge was, in his words, a stupid mistake, since it produces jargon without understanding what alpha means. Verifiable rewards work for math and code because answer keys exist, but in finance and pharma, the data needed to teach reasoning is deliberately withheld. Funds disclose holdings quarterly and lose returns when competitors reverse engineer them; clinical trial disclosure is legally required, yet roughly 30% of firms never do, and in 2026 the FDA publicly reminded over 2,000 sponsors. Frontier labs do not have this data either.

The solution is to hire the user—a senior domain scientist—who curates sources, sharpens prompts, and does the judging, starting from error analysis as the cheapest rung and progressing to preference training. The moat is never the model or infrastructure, as both are commodities; it is domain expertise and proprietary data.

- Applied vertical AI fails when engineers lack domain-specific judgment to evaluate outputs.
- Using a model as a judge is ineffective because it cannot distinguish real correctness from domain jargon.
- Proprietary data is deliberately withheld in finance and pharma, so even frontier labs lack it.
- Hiring a senior domain expert (hiring the user) enables a learning loop from error analysis to preference training.
- The real moat is domain expertise and curated data, not models or infrastructure, which are commodities.
