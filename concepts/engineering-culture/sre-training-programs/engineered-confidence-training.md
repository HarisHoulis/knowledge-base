# Engineering Confidence with Reliable Training Programs

Jennifer Petoff argues that the mantra "you ship it, you own it" only works when engineers have the confidence to face production without fear, and that confidence is not a personality trait but an engineered outcome of focused training. Leaving engineers to "sink or swim" breeds stress, imposter syndrome, and attrition, so organizations should treat training as a reliability-critical asset rather than a passive lecture (GOTO Copenhagen 2026).

Drawing on Google's SRE EDU program, the talk frames training as a continuum of options and tailors guidance by organizational maturity: low-maturity organizations should first address skill gaps, teams starting DevOps should tailor the message, and high-maturity organizations should assess the team mix and find their focus. She also weighs the "what" and "how" of training design and questions outsourcing training to AI.

Petoff offers concrete starting points, including ASSBAT and the ADDIE instructional-design model, and connects training to the service reliability hierarchy. She asks whether more effort is always better and shows before-and-after monitoring evidence, closing with takeaways on engineering confidence and the second edition of the SRE Book.

- Confidence in production is an engineered outcome of targeted training, not a personality trait.
- "Sink or swim" onboarding drives stress, imposter syndrome, and attrition.
- Training should be tailored to organizational maturity: fix skill gaps first, then tailor messaging, then assess team mix and focus.
- Use established frameworks like ADDIE and ASSBAT to structure reliable learning programs.
- Treat training as a reliability-critical asset tied to the service reliability hierarchy.
