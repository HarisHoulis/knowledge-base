# The Making of a Decision

Andrea Magnorsky's GOTO Copenhagen 2026 talk frames architectural solutions as the accumulated outcomes of decisions, and asks how teams actually generate options, choose among them, and verify that a decision solved the original problem. She cites Andrew Harman-Lowe's idea that every decision is a frozen record of the power structures and feedback loops (or lack thereof) that led to it, emphasizing that decisions carry social and organizational context, not just technical content.

She illustrates the variability of context with three scenarios: an acquisition (with uncertainty and anxiety on both sides, plus the difficulty of agreeing on terminology), replacing a widely depended-on library across 25 teams with differing needs, and building quickly under on-call and certification pressure. Though the contexts differ, she argues the underlying decision process is shared.

Drawing on Andrew's book, she outlines that a decision requires first recognizing a decision is needed, then finding options, making the decision, sharing it, and implementing it. Supporting this requires understanding context, system capabilities and limitations, and the problem itself — including agreeing with others on what the problem is — plus awareness of parallel issues, feasibility of implementation, and deliberate feedback collection (knowing what feedback, from whom, and when).

She also stresses knowing available options and people's skills and availability, knowing how to collaborate and with whom, verifying the problem was actually solved, and being able to change the roadmap, block decisions, and convince others to implement. She groups these components into two broad categories: things you know and things you need to be able to change.

- Every decision is a frozen record of the power structures and feedback loops that led to it, so decisions are social as well as technical.
- The decision process: recognize a decision is needed, find options, decide, share, and implement.
- Understanding the problem includes agreeing with others on what the problem actually is.
- Feedback should be collected deliberately — knowing what feedback, from whom, and when.
- Solution components split into things you know and things you need to be able to change.
