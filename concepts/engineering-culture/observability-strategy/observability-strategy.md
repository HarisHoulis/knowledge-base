# First I wrote the wrong book, then I wrote the right book

In this second part of a two-part episode, Charity Majors reveals that after collecting extensive reader feedback on her book about observability, she realized she had written the wrong thing. Her original draft was full of practical advice for observability engineering teams wrestling with implementation challenges, but the emails she received described a deeper, strategic vacuum: broken buying processes, CEOs overruling team decisions, SRE teams stuck in outdated tooling, and a pervasive lack of alignment between engineering and leadership on what observability even is. This forced her to throw out the draft and start over (Charity Majors, "First I wrote the wrong book, then I wrote the right book", https://charity.wtf/p/first-i-wrote-the-wrong-book-then).

The rewritten book is organized for technical decision-makers, starting at the top and descending, explicitly avoiding overloaded technical terms and instead using the language of systems and feedback loops. The new outline includes chapters like "An Open Letter to CTOs: Why Organizational Learning Speed is Now Your Biggest Constraint," "Systems Thinking for Software Delivery," and "The Business Case for Observability." Majors argues that observability is foundational in the AI era because your ability to get returns on AI investments depends on how swiftly you can validate changes and learn from them, which is exactly what observability provides. She also calls out the irony that some of the worst top-down decision-making processes come from companies where CEOs and CTOs are former engineers with outdated confidence in their technical acumen (Charity Majors, URL).

The article also includes a revised table of contents and expresses deep gratitude to the many people who contributed emails, stories, and advice, emphasizing that their input reshaped the book's direction. Majors thanks her co-authors, editor Rita Fernando, and a long list of named contributors, acknowledging that the process was stressful but ultimately led to a much stronger work.

- The initial book draft focused on tactical advice for observability teams, but reader feedback revealed the real problem is a strategic vacuum with no shared alignment between engineering and leadership.
- Majors threw out the original draft and rewrote the book for technical decision-makers, using systems thinking and feedback-loop language to avoid overloaded sales-driven terminology.
- Observability is positioned as critical for AI-native reality: it limits how quickly you can validate changes and learn, directly affecting ROI on AI investments.
- The new table of contents addresses CTOs first, then VPs and distinguished engineers, covering systems thinking, business case, vendor partnerships, and instrumentation.
- The author emphasizes the need for leaders to update their technical priors, noting that former engineers in C-suites often have ten-plus-year-out-of-date confidence.
- The book was significantly shaped by community input, with many named contributors influencing the rewrite.
