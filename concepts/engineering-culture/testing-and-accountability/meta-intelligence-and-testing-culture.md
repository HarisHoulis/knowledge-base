# How Do You Know That? Testing, Meta-Intelligence, and Accountability in AI Systems

In this conversation between Kent Beck and software engineer Beth Andres-Beck, the through-line is curiosity about why people and systems behave as they do. Beth argues that the geek's real superpower is asking why people do what they do, since people always act for reasons — even reasons no one thought through. She illustrates this with a kindergarten story: one child counts to thirty-one, another asks "How do you know that?" The second child is practicing meta-intelligence — learning how the solving gets done — a teachable skill that beats raw smarts.

On testing, Beth notes she didn't write tests for the first seven years of her career, not from laziness but because there were no frameworks, examples, or community support available to her. The lesson: if you want more of a behavior, first ask what's actually stopping it. What finally unlocked testing was design — once she wrote code that was easy to reason about, tests became easy to add. As a first-time manager she built a testing culture by declaring "we're the mobile team, and we write tests," then redirecting code reviews from bugs to missing tests, letting the loop of each test catching a real bug do the persuading.

On AI, Beth contends an agent has no endocrine system — no drive or enthusiasm of its own; the work is done by "us plus the genie," and the caring remains human. She warns that removing humans from the loop doesn't remove accountability, it hides who holds it, and that calling a system "objective" causes people to stop scrutinizing it, letting bias hide. Her self-driving-car anecdote — a car told to relax constraints when stuck that climbed onto the sidewalk — shows systems do exactly what you tell them, not what you mean. Her real fear isn't rogue machines but machines doing exactly what a powerful few instruct.

- Meta-intelligence — asking "how do you know that?" and learning how solving gets done — is teachable and beats raw smarts.
- People rarely skip tests from laziness; find what's actually blocking the behavior, and write testable code first so tests become easy.
- Build a testing culture by declaring the norm, then pointing code reviews at missing tests so each caught bug reinforces the loop.
- AI agents have no drive of their own; removing humans from the loop hides accountability rather than eliminating it.
- Labeling a system "objective" stops scrutiny and lets bias hide; systems do what you tell them, not what you mean.
