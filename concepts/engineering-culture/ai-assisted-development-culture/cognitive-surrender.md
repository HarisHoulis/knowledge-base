# From Chrome DevTools to AI Engineering, with Addy Osmani

In this interview, Addy Osmani discusses his journey from building a web browser at 16 to becoming a Director of Engineering at Google, where he worked on Chrome DevTools, Core Web Vitals, and AI developer experience. He reflects on how publishing free educational materials led to his hiring at Google and how Chrome DevTools evolved from simple debugging tools into a near-IDE for web developers [1]. The conversation also highlights a major cultural shift in engineering: with AI tools making coding easier, even VPs and SVPs at Google now code on weekends, and being accountable on a weekly basis for a top company goal is the biggest difference when moving into a director role [1].

A central theme is the risk of 'cognitive surrender' in AI-assisted development, which Osmani defines as the erosion of comprehension and memory of the codebase and problems being solved. He recommends understanding every major decision an LLM makes, even if reading its entire reasoning process is no longer practical. He also advocates for 'mutual amplification': having AI agents log their decisions and learnings so that both the agent and the engineer improve together. Osmani remains optimistic about software engineering, arguing that AI will expand the total addressable market of builders, but he advises engineers to develop skills beyond coding—product sense, go-to-market awareness, and technical evangelism—because roles are becoming fuzzier and engineers should 'not be just an engineer' [1].

- Cognitive surrender is a key risk of AI-assisted development: engineers must understand the important decisions an LLM makes to avoid losing comprehension.
- Mutual amplification with AI agents involves having the agent log its decisions and learnings, enabling both the engineer and the agent to improve.
- Google's engineering culture has shifted, with senior leaders coding on weekends thanks to AI tools, and being accountable for a top company goal is a defining aspect of director-level roles.
- Software engineering's outlook is positive: easier software creation historically leads to exponentially more software, and AI will expand who can build.
- Engineers should develop non-engineering skills like product sense, go-to-market knowledge, and technical evangelism to succeed as roles become fuzzier.
