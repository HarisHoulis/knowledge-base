# Thoughts on Motivation and My 40-Year Career

Charity Majors' essay is a personal reflection on what has driven her across a 40-year career, from a fundamentalist homeschooled childhood in Idaho to co-founding Honeycomb. She describes leaving home at 15, forging transcripts, working multiple minimum-wage jobs to escape poverty, and eventually dropping out of university to work in San Francisco. Majors credits her survival and success to a deep-seated desire for autonomy, money, and impact, which she contrasts with the small life she was raised for.

A central theme is her internal struggle between her religious upbringing and her ambitions. Majors explains that she initially compartmentalized her beliefs, knowing she was 'going to hell' but hoping to repent later. It wasn't until her late 20s, after intense study and a second ecstatic experience, that she shed her faith. She writes that her career didn't really take off until after that personal reconciliation, as she became able to dedicate her creative energy to tech.

Majors distinguishes her motivation from the 'advancing the industry' framing popularized by Will Larson. For her, the core drive is 'living a meaningful life, and acting with integrity, and building things of enduring value.' She finds intense satisfaction in solving systems problems, even in management and company-building, and argues that 'creation is harder than critique.' She ultimately embraces the ethical complexities of running a startup, seeing work as a place for finding common purpose with ideological opponents.

- Majors' motivation stems from a desire for autonomy and meaningful work, not just a paycheck.
- Her career only took off after resolving a deep personal conflict between her fundamentalist faith and her ambitions.
- She finds joy in building and solving problems, and believes creation is harder than critique.
- Work is a source of liberation, enabling her to support family and community in ways unimaginable in childhood.
