# Pushing software engineering limits with “napkin math”

The article profiles Simon Eskildsen, co-founder of turbopuffer, and his journey from competitive programming in the International Olympiad for Informatics (IOI) to nearly a decade at Shopify. His time at Shopify taught him the value of long tenure, writing software that ages well, and understanding infrastructure deeply. The key superpower he developed is “napkin math”: memorizing approximate numbers for costs, latency, and bandwidth to quickly challenge design decisions and benchmarks. (Source: https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/pushing-software-engineering-limits)

Napkin math involves maintaining a table of figures like DRAM bandwidth, S3 cost per gigabyte, and memory costs, and using flashcards to memorize them. This allowed Simon to spot when a benchmark was orders of magnitude slower than theoretical limits, leading to better engineering decisions. This approach also drove the creation of turbopuffer, a fast and cost-effective search solution, after he realized existing search options were far more expensive than necessary. The article also discusses the origins of turbopuffer, with Cursor as its first customer, and offers insights into why startups raise venture capital: primarily for R&D and growth, not ego. (Source: https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/pushing-software-engineering-limits)

- Napkin math—quick calculations from memorized cost and performance numbers—helps engineers validate or reject benchmark-driven decisions.
- Competitive programming experience, like IOI, can build a foundation for fast, efficient, and memory-aware coding.
- Working at one company for many years lets engineers learn infrastructure nuances and write maintainable software that outlasts large, complex projects.
- Turbopuffer was founded because napkin math proved existing search solutions were far more expensive than theoretically necessary.
- Venture capital should be used to fund R&D and growth, not simply to stroke founders’ egos.
