# Fragments: July  6

Martin Fowler reports from the second Future of Software Development Retreat in Europe, noting a shift from hesitancy to confidence about agentic development. Attendees are now shipping production work with AI agents, and patterns and practices are emerging, though clarity about what is effective remains incomplete. Fowler also highlights the rapid appearance of new terms like 'harness engineering' and a growing concern over token costs, which were barely mentioned at the previous retreat (Fowler, 2026).

A key debate continues over whether architecture and design still matter. Fowler presents two landmark hypotheses: one that advanced LLMs ('The Genie') can handle poorly structured code without human design effort, and another, captured by Laura Tacho, that 'the Venn Diagram of Developer Experience and Agent Experience is a circle.' Adam Tornhill and Mathias Verraes support the latter, arguing that good modularity and naming help both humans and AI agents. Verraes adds that good design is a hedge against dependence on AI, given risks of rising costs, government access blocks, and public opposition (Fowler, 2026).

Fowler engages with Charity Majors' ethics of working with AI, which acknowledges harms like training on stolen data, slop, lack of accountability, and skill atrophy, but argues that renouncing AI use is not ethically superior. Instead, Majors advocates showing up and building better systems. Fowler also cites Gergely Orosz's experience with a fraudulent DMCA takedown and 404 Media's reporting on skyrocketing token costs, which have led companies like Citi, Amazon, and Accenture to throttle AI usage or cut off frontier models (Fowler, 2026).

- Agentic development has moved from aspirational to production, with evidence accumulating and common patterns emerging.
- Architecture and design remain important because AI agents benefit from the same modularity and clear naming that help human developers.
- Token costs are becoming a major operational issue, prompting companies to limit AI usage and seek cost-control measures.
- Ethical engagement with AI involves acknowledging its harms while actively participating in shaping outcomes, rather than withdrawing in purity.
- The retreat also surfaced concerns about dependence on AI, including price volatility, regulatory restrictions, and public resistance.
