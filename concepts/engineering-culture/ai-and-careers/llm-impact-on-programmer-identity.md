# My Obituary for Programming: LLMs and the Loss of the Puzzle-Solving Craft

Simon Willison reflects on how programming became an unusually privileged profession: you were paid to solve tricky logic puzzles, and the harder the puzzle, the more you were paid. Even simple puzzles commanded high pay because ordinary people couldn't do the work, so programmers held leverage—they set strategy, made timelines, and defined what was and wasn't possible.

With LLMs now capable of much of that problem-solving, the author frames the change as a kind of obituary for the craft as it was. The impact depends on what a programmer valued: those who thrive on building working systems and don't care deeply about the problems solved along the way find LLMs a gift that keeps on giving.

For programmers whose enjoyment came primarily from directly solving those problems, the loss is very real. The piece is short and personal, focused on identity and career meaning rather than technical detail.

- Programming was historically lucrative because it combined tricky logic puzzles with scarce expertise and control over strategy and timelines.
- LLMs shift the value toward building working systems rather than personally solving each problem.
- Programmers who enjoy system-building benefit most; those who love direct problem-solving feel a genuine loss.
- The change is framed as an obituary for a particular professional identity, not just a tooling shift.
