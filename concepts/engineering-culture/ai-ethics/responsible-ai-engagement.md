# Is it ethical to use AI?

Charity Majors argues that AI is merely a technology, not uniquely evil, despite acknowledged harms in its creation and use (e.g., training data without permission, labor exploitation, energy costs). She contends that the legal status of AI training is unresolved ("Schrodinger's Law"), but legality does not determine ethics. The author rejects the purity-based approach of avoiding AI entirely, asserting that technologists have a moral responsibility to engage deeply with AI to shape its governance and mitigate harms (Majors, 2026).

Majors distinguishes between harms done in AI's creation and harms enabled by its use, both of which she takes seriously. However, she argues that awareness of these harms should lead to action, not withdrawal. She critiques the "performative denunciation" of AI users as unhelpful and rooted in fundamentalism, citing Alexis Shotwell's "Against Purity" to support the idea that individual purity is less effective than collective engagement (Majors, 2026).

The article offers practical starting points: airing frustrations with AI slop, experimenting with usage boundaries and consent, and having open team conversations about working agreements. Majors also suggests broader actions like supporting compensation for data center neighbors, publicizing tax subsidies, and voting out officials who give them away. Her overarching goal is to make AI "boring"—disciplined, social, and accountable—through collective work, rather than letting it remain a source of fear-driven polarization (Majors, 2026).

- AI is a tool, not inherently evil; focus on disciplined, accountable use.
- Legal status of AI training is unresolved; ethics and law are distinct.
- Engagement, not withdrawal, is the responsible path for technologists.
- Practical steps: open team dialogues, usage boundaries, and addressing specific harms.
- Avoid purity spirals; prioritize collective action and solidarity.
