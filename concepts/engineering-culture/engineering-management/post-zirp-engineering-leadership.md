# The Revised Rules of Engineering Management with Will Larson

In this Pragmatic Engineer interview, Will Larson (CTO of Imprint, author of 'An Elegant Puzzle' and 'Staff Engineer') argues that the unwritten rules of engineering management formed during the 2010s ZIRP era no longer hold. During zero-interest-rate years, hiring and retaining engineers was the core differentiator, which pulled leverage toward engineers and produced perks like relaxed environments, rare 1:1s, and managers told never to make technical decisions. Larson says that era is over: 'hiring is the least interesting way to solve every problem,' and managers must now think about technology and process rather than headcount.

Larson draws on his career at Yahoo, Digg, Uber, Stripe, Carta, and Calm. He recounts Digg v4's failed rewrite (root-caused to Python memory leaks from developers unfamiliar with the language), Yahoo's 'black market' for servers created by a cofounder's personal approval bottleneck, and Stripe's Staff+ engineers who wanted only 'fun' problems. He argues that putting business problems first is now a baseline expectation at senior levels, and that oncall avoidance in the 2010s may have reduced software reliability.

On careers, Larson contends that great engineering executives must be both technologists and bureaucrats, and that the middle-manager path increasingly produces bureaucrats first. He deliberately stepped back from leading 250 people at Carta to 25 at Imprint, and bluntly advises middle managers to re-skill or retire, predicting the engineering leadership profession as we know it won't exist in three years. The tech lead manager role is now in high demand, with EMs managing 12-14 people instead of 4-8, and managers are again expected to be hands-on and make technical decisions. AI is accelerating this shift, making engineers more productive but sometimes less fulfilled, while bringing technical decision-making back to leadership.

- ZIRP-era management practices (hiring as the answer, managers avoiding technical decisions, rare 1:1s) are outdated; hiring is now 'the least interesting way to solve every problem'
- Engineering executives must be both technologists and bureaucrats; the middle-manager path increasingly produces bureaucrats first
- Larson advises middle managers to re-skill or retire, predicting the engineering leadership profession won't exist as we know it in three years
- The tech lead manager role is in high demand as EMs manage larger teams (12-14 vs 4-8) and are expected to stay hands-on
- AI is making engineers more productive but less fulfilled, and is bringing technical decision-making back to leadership
