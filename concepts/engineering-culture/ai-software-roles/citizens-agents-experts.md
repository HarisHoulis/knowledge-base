# Citizens Build, Agents Execute, Experts Govern

Rachel Laycock observes a growing gap between hobbyist AI-built apps and enterprise software. While non-engineers can now create working software over a weekend, production systems raise questions the builders often miss: data protection, dependency failures, maintainability, audits, scale, and early warning of problems. Experienced engineers are becoming more important, not less, because they bring judgment about whether software can be trusted [1].

At FOSE, discussion shifted from coding to design, architecture, governance, and judgment. One team designed a specification, let agents work overnight, and reviewed results in the morning, showing the human role is deciding what good looks like and making trade-offs. When agents generate code quickly, good design matters more, and the scarcity is engineering judgment, not code-writing ability [1].

Laycock's synthesis is that 'Citizens build. Agents execute. Experts govern.' Citizens express ideas, agents handle execution, and experts design guardrails, platforms, and feedback loops that let creativity scale safely. Experienced engineers become leveraged enablers rather than direct builders, because organizations don't run on code—they run on trust [1].

- Building a prototype is different from running enterprise-grade software; trust and risk questions only appear in production.
- AI democratizes building, but the real scarcity becomes good engineering judgment.
- At FOSE, human experts focused on governance, design, and trade-offs while agents executed overnight.
- Engineers' role shifts to creating environments and guardrails, making them more leveraged rather than obsolete.
