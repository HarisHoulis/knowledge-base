# Headed for the Exit: the Great Engineering Leader Career Break

The article reports a notable trend of senior engineering leaders (CTOs, VPs of Engineering, heads of engineering) voluntarily leaving high-status roles or taking prolonged career breaks, often without a next job lined up. Based on interviews with nearly 20 such leaders, the author identifies ten common reasons, including worsening job conditions, startup equity becoming worthless, pressure to become 'AI-native,' long hours, burnout, and the rise of fractional CTO work. A key factor is the shifting value proposition of leadership roles as AI transforms engineering culture and founder expectations become unrealistic (e.g., shipping slop PRs, demanding 20-50% cost cuts).

Equity dilution is a major push factor. The article provides an example where a CTO with 2% equity realizes that due to investor preference stacks (e.g., 2x liquidation preference), the company would need to sell for over $210M for common shareholders to see any return. When growth stalls, such exits become unlikely, making equity worthless and removing a key reason to stay. Additionally, leaders feel they must acquire AI experience to remain relevant, sometimes requiring a career break or IC role, as Charity Majors advises getting 'AI on your resume' at all costs.

Facing these pressures, many leaders conclude that their roles have become 'low ROI.' The article also highlights a counterexample: Matt Boyle describes a rewarding VP Engineering role at Gitpod (later Ona, acquired by OpenAI) by deliberately choosing a company that truly embraces AI-driven change. However, the overarching sentiment is that most companies will fail to become AI-native, leaving leadership roles in a deadlock between frustrated teams and demanding founders, prompting many to exit.

- A growing number of engineering leaders are quitting to take career breaks, driven by unrealistic AI expectations, equity becoming worthless, and diminishing leadership ROI.
- Founder 'slop' (shipping low-quality AI-generated code) and 'AI psychosis' create difficult working conditions and tech debt, eroding quality and accountability.
- Equity value is a major factor: liquidation preferences can make common shares worthless unless the company achieves a large exit, which is unlikely for many VC-funded startups.
- Leaders feel compelled to gain AI experience to stay relevant, sometimes requiring a move to IC roles, consulting, or a career break.
- Most VPEs/CTOs lack the change-management skills needed to transform their orgs into AI-native, leading to frustration and exits.
- The exception: leaders who deliberately choose companies genuinely committed to AI-driven change, like the VP Engineering at Gitpod, can find rewarding roles.
