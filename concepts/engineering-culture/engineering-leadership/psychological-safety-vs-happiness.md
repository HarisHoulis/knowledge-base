# If Your Team Is Happy, Are You Doing a Good Job?

Charity Majors argues that the common management maxim "if your team is happy, you're doing a good job" is a flawed ZIRP-era belief. She traces the drift of psychological safety from its research origins—Google's Project Aristotle (2015) and DORA—where it was defined as "a shared belief held by members of a team that the team is safe for interpersonal risk-taking" and always paired with accountability. The popular understanding has since slid toward "I should feel safe at work" or even "I should never have to feel bad at work," with people citing layoffs, missed promotions, or constructive feedback as threats to their psychological safety. Majors insists psychological safety matters not because it makes people feel safe but because it enables learning, which drives higher performance.

She contends that making a team happy is not the job. A leader who cannot disappoint direct reports or say "no" will never be effective, because growth and learning are inherently uncomfortable, humbling, and vulnerable. She invokes Aristotle's distinction between eudaimonia—flourishing through action and virtue—and hedonic pleasure, noting modern research (Gilbert, Kahneman) confirms we are poor at predicting what will make us happy. Feelings are important and should be acknowledged, but they are not a business outcome to manage to.

Majors also warns that learning is hard to evaluate: activities that look like learning may be the most valuable hour of the month or a total waste, and you cannot tell from the calendar invite. Learning pays off slowly, so leaders should develop a thesis, test it, invest, and measure rather than spray and pray. Finally, she observes that people attribute joy or misery at work to individuals rather than structural factors, giving managers too much credit and blame while letting systems off the hook—and asks Cat whether, when measuring fairness, she looks at people or systems.

- Psychological safety was originally about enabling learning and excellence, and must be paired with accountability; its popular meaning has drifted toward "never feel bad at work."
- Making your team happy is not the job—frustrating people and saying "no" is part of leadership, because growth and learning are uncomfortable.
- Learning is hard to evaluate and pays off slowly; leaders should treat it like an investment with a thesis, tests, and measurement, not spray-and-pray spending.
- People over-attribute workplace joy and misery to individuals rather than structural factors, giving managers too much credit and blame while letting systems off the hook.
