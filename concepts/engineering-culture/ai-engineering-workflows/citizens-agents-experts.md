# Citizens Build, Agents Execute, Experts Govern

Rachel Laycock observes a growing gap between what non-technical executives and experienced engineers think software engineering is, especially after AI enables non-engineers to build working applications over a weekend. While these creations are real software and represent a positive expansion of who can build, they are fundamentally different from enterprise-grade software that must handle security, resilience, compliance, scalability, and long-term maintainability. Laycock argues that the shift from building to governing makes experienced engineers more important, not less, because they supply the judgment needed to decide whether software can be trusted in production.

- AI dramatically increases the number of people who can turn ideas into working software, but building an app is not the same as engineering enterprise software.
- Enterprise software raises questions about data protection, dependency failures, auditability, scalability, and operability—questions that don't appear in demos or weekend builds.
- Experienced engineers become leveraged governors who design guardrails, platforms, and feedback loops, enabling safe and rapid creation by citizens and agents.
- The future software organization is one where citizens build, agents execute, and experts govern, but not via a throw-it-over-the-wall antipattern.
