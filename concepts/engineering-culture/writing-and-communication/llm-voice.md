# LLM Voice: The Uncanny Valley of AI-Written Prose

Martin Fowler describes "LLM Voice" as the growing sense that a piece of writing was produced by an LLM, which triggers an atrophying desire to read on, sometimes to the point of revulsion. He frames this as a human reaction akin to an uncanny valley of prose, and argues that detection tools miss the point: by the time you reach for a tool, the reaction has already happened. He is also wary of his own ability to judge, noting that generational language differences (e.g. "sales motion" or "sharp round-up") are not the same as carbon-versus-silicon tells.

Fowler urges authors to avoid LLM Voice, especially by not letting an LLM write a draft, since even human editing struggles to expel it; accepting rephrasing suggestions is likewise risky. Using LLMs for outlines or structure may be acceptable, but authors should write the prose themselves. He points to his earlier "Say Your Writing" advice—speaking words in your head before writing them down—as even more important now, since good writing depends on practice that surrendering to an LLM undermines.

Beyond style, Fowler worries that delegating writing delegates thinking: writing is how he brings confused thoughts into coherence, and if LLMs become indistinguishable from humans, that loss is more troubling than detectable LLM Voice. He allows exceptions—Jim Highsmith uses an LLM to write despite Parkinson's disease, and non-native English speakers use LLM translation—though he still prefers genuine clunky human prose to smooth but soulless output. Form letters, unread reports, and documentation read only by LLMs are reasonable uses; communicating with humans should let one's humanity show.

He notes a rare case where an LLM first draft was undetectable after professional editing, and states his site policy that AI-assisted writing should be declared, while he himself uses AI only as a search tool with source verification.

- LLM Voice triggers an uncanny-valley reaction in readers that detection tools cannot address, since the reaction precedes any tool use.
- Authors should avoid LLM-drafted prose and rephrasing suggestions; LLM help with outlines or structure may be acceptable, but the prose should be human-written.
- Writing is a tool for thinking, so delegating prose to LLMs risks delegating comprehension as well.
- Exceptions exist for accessibility (e.g. Parkinson's disease), translation, and low-stakes or machine-read documents, but human communication should retain human voice.
- Fowler's site policy asks authors to declare how they used AI in writing; he uses AI only as a verified search tool.
