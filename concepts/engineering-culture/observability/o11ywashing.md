# From Cloudwashing to O11ywashing

Charity Majors argues that the term 'observability' has been so thoroughly co-opted by vendors that it now means little more than traditional monitoring. She recounts a panel where an executive described building custom tooling to understand each customer's experience, not realizing that this is exactly what observability was originally meant to be. The industry has accepted 'traditional observability' as synonymous with availability monitoring, leaving the real problem—understanding service quality from each customer's perspective—unsolved (Majors, 2025).

Majors draws an analogy to 'cloudwashing,' where IBM rebranded mainframes as 'cloud' in 2008. She observes that a similar 'o11ywashing' is happening now as vendors paint monitoring tools with an observability gloss. Since traditional vendors cannot solve the deeper problem, the marketing distortion will only grow. The path forward requires the observability community to tell a better story to engineering executives, focusing on business outcomes rather than technical details like cardinality and dimensionality. Observability, she insists, is a systems problem and the most powerful lever for turning software 'doom spirals' into positive feedback loops. If a tool doesn't help you understand each customer's perspective, it isn't observability—it's just monitoring dressed up in marketing dollars.

- O11ywashing is the practice of rebranding monitoring tools as observability, analogous to cloudwashing.
- Traditional observability (metrics, logs, traces) is really monitoring; true observability means understanding each customer's experience.
- Executives often don't realize the distinction, leading them to build custom solutions for problems observability should solve.
- The observability community must communicate with executives in terms of outcomes, not technical structures.
- Observability is a systems problem, not just an operational one, and can disrupt software doom spirals.
