# Thinking in Platforms: Platform Engineering as Industrial-Era Specialization

Kasper von Grünberg and Luca Galante, co-authors of the book *Thinking in Platforms*, interview each other on the GOTO Book Club about why platform engineering exists and where it came from ([GOTO Book Club](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mfWRYv2V71k)). Their origin story starts with a company building project-management software for NGOs, where the entire backend and codebase depended on one engineer who could effectively blackmail the company for a raise. That dependency was, in their words, a forced first step toward understanding how to build better and more reliable systems. They now run a company, HumaniTech, that helps platform engineers build internal development platforms, mostly in mid-sized and large enterprises, after six or seven years working with hundreds — if not thousands — of platform teams before those teams were even called that.

The central argument of the book is that platform engineering is not a new invention: it has deep roots in the early days of industrialization, in how production worked and factories functioned. Software engineering is extremely young and inexperienced compared with other industries, and the market came to believe DevOps was the best thing ever — shift everything left, everyone does everything. Any other industry, they argue, would call that a terrible idea because production requires specialization. Their point is not that silos are always bad, but that silos can be useful if used correctly.

What made the timing right was the situation in advanced engineering organizations like Airbnb, Spotify and Google: rapidly growing and increasingly complex systems running hybrid, on-premises and cloud, with hundreds of developers onboarding per month in some cases. A company cannot expect its newest developer or a junior front-end specialist to jump in and understand all of that, so some level of platform must exist to protect them from hidden complexity. That, they argue, is the real story behind the emergence of platform engineering.

The community they helped build was a place for people to discuss best practices, what works and what doesn't, and to establish definitions — for example, the definition of the internal developer platform that Kasper first made public, which the episode then digs into.

- Platform engineering has deep historical roots in industrialization and factory production, rather than being a software-native invention.
- The authors' journey began with a single-developer dependency that threatened the whole company, which pushed them toward more reliable systems and eventually to a platform-engineering product company.
- They criticize the DevOps 'everyone does everything' ideal as contrary to the specialization that other industries rely on, and argue silos can be useful when used correctly.
- Internal developer platforms emerged to shield a fast-growing, increasingly complex developer population (hybrid, on-prem, cloud) from hidden complexity at scale.
