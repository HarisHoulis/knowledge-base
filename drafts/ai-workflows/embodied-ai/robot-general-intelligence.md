# Robotics Has Been Stuck for 70 Years

AI has made dramatic progress over the last five years in speech, audio, and video, and many — including Jensen Huang with his talk of "physical AI" — expect robotics to be next, with social feeds implying home robots are imminent. Deepak Pathak argues the opposite framing: robotics has been "almost here" for the last 70 years, and progress is far smaller than the hype suggests.

- Robotics demos have looked essentially unchanged for decades: a 1960s MIT copying demonstration, a 1957 leader-follower teleoperation rig, a juggling/foosball humanoid from before deep learning, and a Berkeley table-clearing robot run on a single GPU.
- The teleoperation data-collection scheme used in labs and companies today is the same leader-follower principle demonstrated in 1957.
- Moravec's paradox explains the gap: difficult tasks for humans (math, Olympiad problems) are easy for computers, while easy tasks (climbing stairs) are hard — and robotics sits on the hard side.
- Robotics has historically been treated as a hardware problem, which is why everything around it evolved while robotics itself stayed at the same level.
- A computer beat Kasparov at chess in the 1990s, yet no robot can pick up chess pieces and place them on any chessboard.
