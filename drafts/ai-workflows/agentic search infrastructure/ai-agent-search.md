# The Search Engine for the Agentic Web — Will Bryk, Exa

Will Bryk presents Exa as "the search engine for AI agents" and argues that search is shifting from human use to agent use. Web searches per day were all human until around the 2020s; in 2026, AI searches are expected to exceed human searches, and in the next few years AI systems may search a thousand times more than humans (Bryk, 2026). Exa reports serving over 5,000 companies and 400,000 developers, powering agents such as Cursor for technical documentation and news, HubSpot for go-to-market company lists, and financial agents that need financial data (Bryk, 2026).

The core problem Exa targets is "misinformed anarchy": the web is enormous, on the order of a trillion pages, and messy, so people need tools that synthesize and filter information (Bryk, 2026). Bryk argues Google functions more like a recommendation engine than a database of the world's information. Examples include searching "shirts without stripes" and still getting striped shirts, and queries like "find me everyone in Singapore who works on AI search and any blog post or research paper they've written" that Google cannot answer as a database result (Bryk, 2026). He also says users do not trust Google to return the most important US news across all media (Bryk, 2026).

The consequence, according to Bryk, is that no one has a complete understanding of anything and people remain poorly informed (Bryk, 2026). Exa's pitch is therefore not just better ranking but search built for agents that need high-quality, structured retrieval over the whole web.

- AI-driven web searches are projected to overtake human searches in 2026 and grow to roughly 1,000x human search volume in coming years.
- Exa positions itself as the first search engine built for AI agents, serving 5,000+ companies and 400,000+ developers.
- Agent use cases include coding agents like Cursor, go-to-market agents like HubSpot, and financial agents needing financial data.
- Exa aims to counter "misinformed anarchy" by providing database-like search over the messy, trillion-page web.
- Bryk argues Google is a recommendation engine, not a reliable database, citing failed retrieval for structured queries and important news.
