# The Cost of Accessibility in AI: Not Wasting Tokens on Unusable Products

Jemima Abu argues that accessibility—the ability of a product to be used by anyone regardless of disability—is especially critical in the era of "vibe coding" and AI agents that write most of our code. She cites WebAIM research showing that 96% of the homepages of the world's million most popular websites have some kind of accessibility issue, and notes that the common excuse for this is that accessibility takes too much time. Her counter-argument is that now that AI writes most of our code, making things accessible is actually easier, and that inaccessible websites cost more time and money.

To test this, Abu ran an experiment on her work codebase, giving agents a simple prompt with no instructions to implement a Figma design, twice: once without any accessibility skills and once using a skill she wrote. She reports that not only was it faster for the agent to build an accessible interface, but the cost was reduced by 58%, and it took three times less time to complete the prompt using the accessibility skill. She is careful to note this was a one-time experiment in an unconfined environment and cannot serve as empirical data proving the claim.

Abu frames AI agents as super-intelligent tools that are nonetheless only "three years old" and vulnerable to influence, like a baby who will believe the sky is red if told enough times. Drawing an analogy to the Netflix show "Old Enough," where toddlers are prepared with clear instructions before their first errand, she argues that responsible developers must provide the right skills and guidance so agents create the products we expect. Without guidance, human biases—such as the "it works for me" mentality of developers who don't test with screen readers or in low light—are translated into AI biases, and agents absorb that mentality from the codebase.

- 96% of the homepages of the world's million most popular websites have some kind of accessibility issue, per WebAIM research cited by Abu.
- In Abu's experiment, using an accessibility skill reduced the cost of building an accessible interface by 58% and took three times less time than building without it.
- Abu cautions the experiment was one-time and conducted in an unconfined environment, so it cannot serve as empirical proof.
- AI agents are vulnerable to influence and absorb human biases like the "it works for me" mentality from existing codebases.
- Providing agents with the right skills and guidance, like preparing a toddler for their first errand, simplifies the task and yields the intended product.
