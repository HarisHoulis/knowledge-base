# Design Engineering with Maggie Appleton

In this Pragmatic Engineer Podcast episode, Gergely Orosz interviews Maggie Appleton, a staff research engineer at GitHub Next who prototypes new ways for engineers to collaborate with AI. Appleton came to tech via cultural anthropology — she turned down a post-graduation job inventing torture techniques for the US army — and argues that software is built by people, so relationships matter. She learned frontend engineering by illustrating React tutorials over four years at Egghead, rising to art director, because drawing React components, useEffect, and JavaScript functions required understanding them.

Appleton frames design and software engineering as related forms of problem solving that differ mainly in their materials. She stresses that nomenclature matters: inventing the names and verbs for new things that people will adopt is hard work — easy for an online sneakers store, harder for a new AWS product. She also describes a failed 2021 attempt at an AI research interface, built a year before ChatGPT, where researchers rejected infinite canvases and Notion-like composable documents in favor of the simple tables they already knew. The lesson: start from a familiar primitive, even when innovating.

Her process is deliberately physical and small-scale. She often starts projects by sketching by hand, finding it faster than describing an idea to a tool like Claude Code, and notes that a notebook sketch is still there the next day, whereas an idea buried dozens of prompts deep is hard to recover. She builds her own "Jigs" — named after the woodworking device — by asking a coding agent for prototypes with sliders and color pickers so she can tweak in real time, effectively a personal Figma. At work she no longer reads the code on prototype PRs; once she knows what to build she writes a detailed spec including how the agent will verify its work.

On working with agents, she argues planning is currently a bad experience: an agent fires off hundreds of A/B/C choices and by question 20 your brain shuts down and you just accept the recommended option. She coined "capability gaslighting" for models that impress users as experts and then fail the same task the next day, urging vigilance. She believes new artifacts are needed for humans and agents to meet in the middle — agents live in weights, models, skills and MCPs, while humans live in physicality, texture, light and materials — and she is frustrated that agents cannot look over her shoulder at her notebook. Her advice to engineers: treat the AI agent as a patient tutor for design skills like line height, sidebars, and line length.

- Start from a familiar primitive even when innovating — researchers rejected infinite canvases and Notion-like documents in favor of simple tables they already knew.
- Physical tools matter: hand sketching beats prompting an agent because the idea persists in a notebook rather than getting buried dozens of prompts deep.
- Appleton builds personal "Jigs" — agent-generated prototypes with sliders and color pickers — and no longer reads the code on prototype PRs, instead writing detailed specs that include how the agent verifies its work.
- Planning with agents breaks down under too much text; after enough A/B/C questions users just accept the recommended option.
- Beware "capability gaslighting": frontier models can convince users they are experts and then fail the same task the next day.
- Engineers can use agents as patient tutors to pick up product design skills like line height, sidebar layout, and line length.
