# Confessions of an Unrepentant Slop Snob: When AI Use Degrades Trust

Charity Majors describes descending into two warring camps at her company: half the staff felt their attention was abused by twenty-page AI-generated docs and "Claude says..." replies, while the other half felt they were delivering better outcomes faster and that holdouts simply refused to update their workflows (charity.wtf). She initially resolved the conflict with a ruthlessly outcome-oriented stance — tools are tools, customers care about product quality, not how it was made — but noticed she was simultaneously developing a "violent disgust reflex" toward AI-generated text, eventually deleting newsletters, closing tabs, and judging authors who sent her slop.

She reconciles the contradiction by classifying what language is doing. Language and writing serve either functional or relational jobs: structured data, formal proofs, or shared documents and diffs are functional, and substituting AI there is "wholly acceptable." But where value derives from a specific person having thought or felt something — personal opinions, performance reviews, a human-to-human outreach — AI-generated text "registers as a violation," because people expect to hear your voice (charity.wtf). Majors argues the relevant question is not how much AI was used but in which contexts, and secondarily whether its use was disclosed and consented to.

What makes AI uniquely corrosive is its "slick, sycophantic, uncanny valleyness" — human-like but not human, which is more alienating than plainly automated messages. Companies are both outcome machines and collectives of people, and while lines of code are indifferent to their origin, people are intensely attuned to the language used with them; relationship maintenance cannot be automated. She contends the effect can be compensated for, but not by denying it and forcing AI-generated happy birthdays and performance reviews on people (charity.wtf).

Her conclusion is that old communication norms (like "this was written by a person") no longer hold and new ones are not yet agreed upon, so companies benefit from writing down their expectations. Honeycomb's resulting AI Norms and Values series, based on a personal-to-functional scale across personal opinion, professional opinion, artifacts, and code, is her attempt to supply such a convention (charity.wtf).

- Writing serves functional or relational jobs; AI substitution is acceptable in functional contexts (structured data, proofs, shared docs) but degrades trust in personal/relational ones like opinions and performance reviews.
- The key variable is context, not volume: which situations AI is used in, plus whether its use was disclosed and consented to.
- AI-generated text is uniquely alienating because of its human-like-but-not-human register; people react more harshly to it than to plainly automated messages.
- Requests for a personal response answered with chatbot output feel like a violation of the relationship, and recipients often overestimate how much of the content was their own.
- Because old norms about authorship have collapsed, explicitly documented company conventions for AI communication help reduce friction.
