# Why and How I Started Public Speaking

Kent C. Dodds got into tech public speaking when, as a BYU Information Systems student, he heard about the UtahJS meetup on JavaScript Jabber, saw an open speaker slot, and offered to present his open source project geniejs—so his first meetup attendance was also his first talk. He says meetup organizers are often eager to give slots because monthly speakers are surprisingly hard to find (source).

He traces his comfort with speaking to many childhood and adolescent opportunities: church primary programs, seminary, family home evening lessons, high school speech class, stage performance such as piano, choirs, and show choir, and a church mission. These experiences made public speaking fun rather than terrifying; he still gets nervous, but practice helps turn nervous energy into audience engagement (source).

He later moved to conference speaking partly to attend more conferences, since speakers normally have expenses paid. He searched CFPs, wrote a dozen proposals, submitted widely, and was accepted at Jfokus in Stockholm, which also led to his first paid workshop and later an egghead.io gig. He eventually pulled back because too much speaking negatively affected family and work (source).

His stated reasons for speaking now include joy of sharing and connecting, travel, industry recognition for financial stability, promoting technologies he likes, and solidifying what he learns. His advice: spend a ton of time in front of people, do not fear rejection, and keep asking—his early conference acceptance rate was about 5%, while meetup proposals were about 95% (source).

- Start with local meetups: organizers often need speakers, so a first meetup can become a first speaking slot.
- Conference speaking can help fund attendance because speakers normally have expenses paid; submit many CFPs and expect low acceptance rates.
- Prior speaking practice reduces fear, but nervousness never fully goes away; practice turns nervous energy into audience engagement.
- Benefits of speaking include sharing knowledge, travel, recognition, community promotion, and solidifying learning.
- Scale back if speaking starts harming family and work; be more selective about conferences.
