# The Past, Present & Future of Programming Languages (Kevlin Henney, YOW! 2025)

Kevlin Henney's talk examines which programming languages endure, framed by the admission that forecasting his own field has been "on a coin flip" — sometimes right, sometimes wrong (Henney, YOW! 2025). He opens by invoking Yoda's wisdom as a warning that any claim about the future will be wrong at some level, but says he will try to provide evidence rather than rely on luck this time.

His central evidence comes not from a technical source but from Anthony Doerr's 2021 novel *Cloud Cuckoo Land*, which spans four centuries and five point-of-view characters, from the fall of Constantinople to a post-apocalyptic 22nd century (Henney, YOW! 2025). Doerr wrote the book roughly 2016–2020, with final drafts signed off in 2020 — so he could not know what 2025 or 2030 would actually bring, yet he still managed to insert a pandemic-era detail (a character removing a face mask) in the final drafts.

The relevant passage concerns Seymour, a character in the Idaho State Correctional Institution who spends every hour he can in the computer lab and "has learned Excel, AutoCAD, Java, C++, and Python," taking comfort in the clear logic of code — input and output, instruction and command (Henney, YOW! 2025). Henney asks what languages a writer would put in that story in 2020, and argues that Java, C++, and Python were a safe bet then; they remain a safe bet in 2025, and he sets up the question of whether that bet holds for 2030. He notes the talk has existed in 45-, 60-, and 90-minute versions, and that it reaches back to the 1977 Turing Award speech (published in *Communications of the ACM*, with a 1978 copyright).

- Henney frames forecasting programming-language futures as roughly a coin flip, and aims to ground his predictions in evidence rather than claimed expertise.
- Anthony Doerr's *Cloud Cuckoo Land* (2021), written largely 2016–2020, includes a 2020s character who learns Excel, AutoCAD, Java, C++, and Python — a snapshot of what a novelist considered durable languages.
- Henney treats Java, C++, and Python as a safe bet both for 2025 and plausibly 2030, which is the argument the rest of the talk develops.
- The talk traces back to the 1977 Turing Award speech as its historical anchor.
