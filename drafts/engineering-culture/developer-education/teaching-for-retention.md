# How I Teach

In "How I Teach," Kent C. Dodds explains how he changed his programming teaching based on *Make It Stick: The Science of Successful Learning*. He argues against lecturing or firehosing information, focusing instead on methods that help learners retain material long-term (Dodds, "How I Teach").

He applies four main techniques. First, "generation" or desirable difficulties: learners attempt exercises before concepts are taught, often with emoji guides, so they make and test assumptions. Correct assumptions produce dopamine; incorrect ones produce emotion that helps solidify memory. Second, interleaving: mixing practice and having exercises build on one another so learners revisit prior material while learning new things. Third, elaboration: after every exercise, learners write what they learned in their own words via a feedback form, which also gives Dodds material to improve; by the article's writing he had 3.6k responses since early 2017. Fourth, active retrieval: workshop material is open source, and he encourages attendees to go through it again a week later to interrupt natural forgetting.

Dodds concludes that teaching and learning are hard, but learning how to learn can make someone a more effective learner and teacher. Limited workshop time means not every principle can be fully applied, but adapting the *Make It Stick* principles has been his most effective way to teach for retention (Dodds, "How I Teach").

- Use generation/desirable difficulties: have learners attempt exercises before teaching concepts so they struggle, make assumptions, and retain better through dopamine or corrective emotion.
- Interleave practice by mixing what is learned and building exercises on one another.
- Use elaboration: after every exercise, ask learners to write what they learned in their own words; this aids retention and provides feedback to the instructor.
- Encourage active retrieval by making material available and having learners review it again a week later to interrupt forgetting.
- Teaching and learning require deliberate effort; applying learning-science principles improves retention even if time limits prevent perfect application.
